\begin{abstract}
MEDORA is a proposed integrated clinic-management and electronic medical-record (EMR) system for outpatient clinics and small healthcare facilities. It addresses workflow fragmentation across patient registration, appointment scheduling, check-in, clinical documentation, prescriptions, service execution, billing and operational reporting. The proposed solution centres on a shared patient and encounter record, explicit workflow states, role-based access control and an audit trail for sensitive actions. This report develops the business model, domain concepts, business rules, functional and non-functional requirements, use-case model and traceability needed to guide the subsequent system design and evaluation. The scope is deliberately limited to internal clinic operations; clinical decision-making, insurer settlement, national interoperability and production compliance certification remain outside the current project boundary.

\vspace{0.4em}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} electronic medical records, clinic management, outpatient workflow, role-based access control, system analysis and design.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction and Problem Definition}

\subsection{Background and Existing System}
Healthcare delivery is becoming a coordination problem as much as a clinical one. Across countries, providers are expected to offer safer, more continuous and more patient-centred care while dealing with rising demand, constrained staff capacity and increasingly complex information flows. The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that approximately one in ten patients is harmed during health care and that more than half of this harm is preventable; medication-related harm accounts for a substantial share of the preventable cases \citep{WHO2023Safety}. These figures do not imply that software can replace clinical judgement. They do, however, show why reliable identification, complete records, clear hand-offs and traceable actions are system-level concerns rather than administrative details.

Digital health is therefore moving from a collection of isolated applications towards an integrated operating capability. The WHO describes digital health as a means of aligning organizational, human and technological resources around better health outcomes, not simply as the deployment of another application \citep{WHO2021Digital}. Yet digitalisation can reproduce the same fragmentation when applications are implemented as disconnected, supply-driven tools; WHO identifies integration over isolation, data governance and protection of privacy as essential conditions for useful digital transformation \citep{WHO2021DataGovernance,WHO2024DigitalTransformation}. In a clinic, this distinction is important: an appointment calendar, a patient database and a billing screen may each work in isolation while the overall journey still depends on repeated data entry, informal communication and manual reconciliation. The result is a fragile process in which a patient can be correctly registered in one place but remain invisible to the next person who must act.

The persistence of legacy workflows is not unique to one country. The OECD reports that 75\% of global fax traffic is used for medical services and that evidence-based innovations can take as long as 17 years to become routine practice, illustrating the distance between available technology and dependable day-to-day adoption \citep{OECD2023DigitalHealth}. For a small clinic, the implication is practical: the priority is not to buy the most sophisticated technology, but to design a coherent system that staff can use consistently and that can exchange trustworthy data when the organization grows.

The Vietnamese context makes this challenge particularly tangible. Viet Nam entered the ageing phase in 2011; people aged 60 and over represented 11.9\% of the population in 2019, and this share is projected to exceed 25\% by 2050 \citep{UNFPA2021Ageing}. An ageing population increases the need for repeated visits, longitudinal clinical histories and coordination across services. At the point of care, operational friction is already measurable. In a study of 7,931 outpatient visits at Thong Nhat Hospital, the mean total waiting time was 104.1 minutes, with departments handling roughly 150--250 patients per day while clinic capacity and staffing remained comparatively stable \citep{Nguyen2018Waiting}. A separate study across Vietnamese health facilities reported average waits of 32.58 minutes at district hospitals and 11.58 minutes at commune health centres before patients met clinical staff \citep{Nguyen2020Outpatient}. These are not merely comfort issues: long queues consume staff time, obscure workload, increase opportunities for transcription errors and weaken patients' confidence in the service.

\subsection{Problem Statement and Motivation}
The need for an electronic medical-record system is visible in three connected indicators. First, outpatient flow is slow: a study of 7,931 visits at a Vietnamese national hospital reported a mean total waiting time of 104.1 minutes, while departments handled approximately 150--250 patients per day without proportional capacity growth \citep{Nguyen2018Waiting}. A broader study reported average waits of 32.58 minutes at district hospitals and 11.58 minutes at commune health centres before patients met clinical staff \citep{Nguyen2020Outpatient}. Second, patient-safety risk remains material: the WHO estimates that one in ten patients is harmed in health care and that more than half of this harm is preventable \citep{WHO2023Safety}. Third, medication errors alone are estimated to cost about US\$42 billion globally each year \citep{WHO2017Medication}.

These figures do not prove that every clinic has the same performance. They establish why the workflow deserves system-level attention. When identity, appointments, clinical notes, orders and payment are split across paper forms, spreadsheets or isolated applications, the following problems become likely:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
    \item duplicate patient records and repeated demographic entry;
    \item appointment, room or staff conflicts that are discovered too late;
    \item incomplete visibility of allergies, previous encounters, current medication or open orders;
    \item unclear responsibility when an order moves from prescription to execution;
    \item silent overwriting of signed information and weak evidence of who changed it; and
    \item limited management visibility into waiting, workload, service progress and payment status.
\end{itemize}

WHO identifies fragmented data governance and weak exchange mechanisms as barriers to timely, high-quality use of health data \citep{WHO2021DataGovernance}. MEDORA addresses this coordination gap by providing one patient identity, one encounter context and explicit workflow states across authorized roles. Its ``smart'' behaviour is deliberately bounded: the system applies rules, warnings, validation and audit trails at the right step, while diagnosis and treatment decisions remain with qualified clinicians.

The architecture is part of the motivation. Separating presentation, application rules, domain entities and persistence prevents a new screen from bypassing clinical or access-control rules. Explicit services and state transitions make double-booking prevention, signed-record amendment, authorization and auditability testable. The result is a system that is safer to operate today and easier to extend tomorrow, rather than a set of screens that merely digitizes existing fragmentation.

\subsection{Project Objectives and Success Criteria}
This project proposes MEDORA, an integrated clinic-management and electronic medical-record system for a clinic or small healthcare facility. Its objective is to turn a fragmented sequence of tasks into a controlled, observable and reusable workflow. The project pursues five linked outcomes:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
    \item one stable patient identity and one longitudinal record across visits;
    \item coordinated appointments, queues, rooms and staff;
    \item structured support for examination, diagnosis, prescriptions and service orders;
    \item auditable handling of sensitive changes and role-based access to health data; and
    \item operational dashboards and reports that support evidence-based management.
\end{itemize}

Success is specified using SMART criteria so that the prototype can be evaluated without overstating clinical impact. During the final test cycle, against a documented baseline workload, MEDORA will:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
    \item reduce median registration-to-completion time by at least 25\% for the representative outpatient scenario;
    \item accept zero double-bookings for the same doctor, room or constrained resource across 100\% of the concurrency test cases;
    \item achieve at least 95\% completion of configured mandatory encounter fields across the tested completed visits;
    \item record an actor, timestamp, action and affected entity for 100\% of tested sensitive events, including clinical amendments, prescription changes, exports and permission changes; and
    \item demonstrate that 100\% of negative authorization test cases are rejected at the API/server boundary, while the principal happy-path workflow completes successfully in the agreed test environment.
\end{itemize}

Each target is specific to a defined workflow, measurable through test evidence, achievable for the course-project scope, relevant to the stated pain points and time-bound to the final evaluation cycle. The figures are prototype acceptance targets, not claims of production performance; a real deployment would first establish a site-specific baseline and then revalidate them with clinical and operational stakeholders.

\subsection{System Scope, Assumptions, and Constraints}
MEDORA is an internal web system for a clinic or small healthcare facility. It supports the outpatient journey from patient identification and appointment or walk-in reception to check-in, screening, consultation, prescription or service order, execution, encounter closure and payment status.

Receptionists manage identity, appointments and queues; nurses record screening; doctors own clinical decisions; technicians and medicine staff record actual execution; cashiers record financial events; managers, administrators and auditors supervise operations, configuration and accountability. The system preserves the difference between planned and completed work: an appointment is not an encounter, a prescription is not a medicine event, an order is not a result, and an invoice is not a payment.

The MVP focuses on internal operations, basic billing, reporting and governance. Patient self-service, external diagnostic systems, insurer settlement and advanced inventory are later-stage extensions. Every sensitive action is constrained by role, scope and workflow state; signed notes are amended rather than overwritten, and concurrent booking must not create a conflict.

\subsection{Proposed Solution and System Overview}
MEDORA is a clinic operating system built around one shared patient and encounter record, not a collection of unrelated modules. Its central unit of work is the outpatient encounter. A Patient may have many Appointments and Encounters; an Encounter is the controlled context for clinical notes, vital signs, diagnoses, prescriptions and procedure orders; an order may consume a scheduled ResourceBooking and later receive an execution result; and an Invoice and Payment record the financial consequence without changing the clinical history. Attachments, notifications and AuditLogs are linked to the relevant business object rather than stored as anonymous files or messages.

The solution uses four cooperating layers:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
    \item \textbf{Presentation:} role-specific workspaces for reception, nursing, consultation, service execution, cashier operations and management.
    \item \textbf{Application:} use cases such as registration, booking, check-in, consultation, prescription, service scheduling, result recording, encounter closure and payment.
    \item \textbf{Domain:} rules for availability, permissions, state transitions, signed-record amendments and clinical warnings.
    \item \textbf{Data:} persistent patient, encounter, order, resource, invoice and audit entities with integrity constraints and history.
\end{itemize}

Each workflow step has an explicit state and accountable actor. Backend authorisation, controlled attachments and audit history protect the record while the modular structure leaves a clear path for later portal, diagnostic and interoperability extensions.

\section{Business Analysis and Domain Modeling}

\subsection{Business Context and Operational Overview}
MEDORA is an internal web system for an outpatient clinic or small healthcare facility. It coordinates one outpatient episode from patient identification and appointment handling to consultation, service execution, payment status and operational reporting. Its central design principle is continuity: every authorised role works from the same patient and encounter context rather than from disconnected records.

Four distinctions protect that continuity. An Appointment reserves time, while an Encounter represents the visit that actually occurs. A prescription is a clinical decision; dispensing or administration is a later, accountable event. A service order is not an execution result. An Invoice records charges, whereas a Payment or refund records a financial transaction. These distinctions prevent the system from reporting planned work as completed work.

The MVP covers internal clinic operations, basic billing, reporting and governance. Patient self-service, external laboratory or imaging interfaces, insurer settlement and other integrations are later-stage capabilities.

The workflow is governed by five control points:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em]
    \item Search and duplicate checks occur before a new patient record is created.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}

\subsection{Summary of Achievements}
The current deliverable establishes a clinic-focused business and requirements baseline, a traceable use-case model, and a set of behavioral models for appointment booking, check-in, clinical-note integrity, prescription safety, service execution, encounter closure, and payment history. It also defines a practical test plan with expected outcomes and explicit status reporting. These are analysis and design achievements; they do not demonstrate that MEDORA has been implemented, deployed, or tested.

\subsection{Limitations of the Current System}
There is no application implementation in the repository, so MEDORA should be described as a specified/proposed system rather than an operational product. No source modules, persistent schema, API, user interface, deployment topology, test execution evidence, or user feedback are available. The use-case, activity, sequence, and state diagrams express intended behavior; they remain subject to policy confirmation and later reconciliation with code. Performance, availability, recovery, and success-rate values are targets only. Clinical rule sources, data retention, consent, approval thresholds, multi-branch operation, and portal publication remain open decisions.

\subsection{Future Improvements and Extensions}
Future work should proceed in evidence-producing stages:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em]
    \item Confirm clinic policies for authorization, clinical safety, record correction, finance, retention, and lifecycle transitions.
    \item Decide and document the architecture, persistence model, deployment assumptions, and mapping from approved components to source modules.
    \item Implement a testable MVP for identity, appointment/check-in, encounter documentation, role/scope enforcement, and audit evidence.
    \item Extend the MVP with clinician-approved prescription and service workflows, then basic finance with separate, immutable transaction history.
    \item Establish automated and negative-authorization tests; measure quality targets using synthetic data and a recorded environment; capture privacy-redacted UI/API evidence and usability/recovery results.
    \item Add the patient portal or external integrations only after scope, contracts, security controls, and operational ownership are approved.
\end{enumerate}

This sequence keeps the report aligned with the project's clinic scope and makes each future claim auditable against source code, test results, or operational evidence.
